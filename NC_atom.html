The atom

The picture of the atom evolved through 5 models, shifting from a simple indivisible sphere to a complex quantum probability cloud:

(1) The Solid Sphere Model of John Dalton (1808, 1810, 1827) who proposed the first modern scientific theory of the atom based on chemical experiments. Here, matter consists of indivisible and indestructible solid spheres ('billiard'(!) balls)1, but it could not explain electrical charges or the existence of smaller subatomic particles.

(2) The so called 'Plum Pudding' Model of J. J. Thomson (1897a, b, 1907), discovering the negatively charged electron using cathode ray tubes. Now, atoms are spheres of diffuse positive charge with negatively charged electrons embedded randomly throughout. However, the model failed to explain that positive charge is concentrated rather than spread out.

(3) The Nuclear Model of Ernest Rutherford (1911). In his famous gold foil experiment, he indicated that the atom is mostly empty space, with nearly all its mass and positive charge concentrated in a dense central nucleus, orbited by electrons. According to classical physics, however, orbiting electrons continuously lose energy - spiral into the nucleus - making the atom unstable.

(4) The Planetary Model of Niels Bohr (1913a, b, c, 1928, 1935), that refined Rutherford, applying Max Planck’s (1901)2 early quantum theory, holds that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed paths (energy shells) without radiating energy, jumping between levels, absorbing or emitting amounts of light. Even though the model predicted hydrogen, it still failed for multi-electron atoms.

Finally (5), the Quantum Mechanical Model. Erwin Schrödinger (1926a, b, c, d, e), building on De Broglie (1925)3 and Heisenberg (1925, 19274) used wave equations to describe electron behavior. So, electrons exist in three-dimensional regions of probability, the orbitals or electron clouds.

As later refinement, James Chadwick (1932) discovered the neutron, completing the modern picture of the atomic nucleus with protons and neutrons (c.f. Schrödinger, 1951).

1: Ratio deduction, by comparing different compounds formed from the exact same two elements (e.g. carbon monoxide vs. carbon dioxide), he noticed the masses always combined in small, whole-number ratios, deducting that this could only happen if elements were made of indivisible, discrete units.
2: Planck constant, das Planck'sche Wirkungsquantum $h=6.62607015 \cdot 10^{-34} J \cdot s$ beschreibt das konstante Verhältnis zwischen der Energie eines Quants (z.B. Photon) und dessen Frequenz.
3: Wave-particle duality for matter, as light exhibits both, wave and particle, properties, particles such as electrons have a wave nature.
4: Introducing the uncertainty principle, die Heisenberg'sche Unschärferelation, welche besagt, dass zwei komplementäre Eigenschaften eines Quantensystems (Ort und Impuls) nicht gleichzeitig beliebig genau bestimmt werden können.
